Pressure Washing Price Per Square Foot: Build the Rate From Your Costs

Searching for a single pressure-washing rate per square foot is tempting, but copying somebody else's number can leave you underpriced. The safer method is to calculate the selling price your job actually needs, then use the resulting square-foot rate as a quoting benchmark.

The cost-first formula

Required selling price = Total job cost ÷ (1 − target profit margin)

Then:

Effective price per sq ft = Required selling price ÷ Surface area

Your total job cost should include more than chemicals. At minimum, account for loaded labor, chemicals and consumables, fuel and travel, equipment allocation and job-level overhead.

What changes the square-foot rate?

1. Job size

Fixed costs are spread across more square footage on a large project, so a large job can sometimes support a lower effective rate than a tiny driveway while still producing more profit.

2. Surface condition

Heavy organic growth, oil, rust, oxidation, gum, paint or other treatment-heavy conditions increase chemical use and labor time.

3. Access and risk

Steep grades, difficult hose runs, roof work, lifts, traffic control, water limitations and protected landscaping can materially change cost and risk.

4. Travel and setup

A 20-minute wash can still require a meaningful trip, setup and breakdown. That is why a minimum service charge matters.

5. Commercial requirements

Large commercial work can add insurance requirements, lifts, water recovery, after-hours scheduling, additional crew time or administrative work. Do not assume a residential rate transfers cleanly to commercial jobs.

Worked example

Suppose a 2,500-square-foot job requires six crew hours. After loaded labor, chemicals, travel, equipment and overhead, your true cost is $330. If your target gross margin is 35%, the required selling price is:

$330 ÷ 0.65 = $507.69

The effective rate is:

$507.69 ÷ 2,500 = about $0.20 per sq ft

The point is not that $0.20 is the “right” market rate. The point is that your square-foot number was produced by the economics of the job rather than guessed first.

Use a minimum price

If the cost-first calculation produces a number below your minimum profitable trip charge, quote the minimum instead. Your minimum should reflect the cost of getting a crew and equipment to the property, setting up, performing the smallest practical job and returning without giving away the fixed portion of the work.

Margin is not markup

A 35% markup on $330 produces a selling price of $445.50. That is only about a 26% gross margin. If your goal is a 35% margin, divide cost by 0.65 instead. Confusing markup with margin is one of the easiest ways to underprice service work.

Pressure washing pricing FAQ

Should every job use the same price per square foot?

No. The effective rate should change when labor intensity, chemicals, access, risk, travel or equipment requirements change.

Should I quote by the square foot or by the job?

You can calculate internally by square foot and still present the customer with a single project price. That keeps your costing disciplined without turning every proposal into a rate negotiation.

How do I know whether I am undercharging?

Track actual labor time and direct job costs after completion. Compare them with what you estimated. If real cost repeatedly exceeds the assumptions behind your quotes, raise the rate or change the estimating inputs.
